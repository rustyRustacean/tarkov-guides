## Understanding Peeking: Seeing Before You Commit

Peeking in Tarkov isn’t about fancy movement, it’s about information. Players use peeks to spot enemies before committing to irreversible pushes like a wide swing. The more information you gather without dying, the stronger your follow-up decisions become. Done correctly, peeking lets you see them first, shoot first, or disengage entirely.

### Understanding Right-Hand vs Left-Hand Peeking

Before diving into the techniques, it’s critical to understand how your character’s perspective affects peeking. Tarkov’s camera is positioned over your **right eye**, and your weapon is mounted on the **right shoulder**. This means that when you peek from the right side of cover, you only need to expose your weapon and a small portion of your head to see or shoot. On the other hand, left-hand peeks are inherently worse. Because the gun and view model are both right-sided, you must expose most of your upper body before your weapon clears the wall. This makes left-hand peeks slower, riskier, and easier to punish.

Whenever possible, position yourself so that your engagements occur from a right-hand corner. If you’re caught on a left-hand side, it’s often better to reposition and regain the right-hand advantage.

### Lean Peeking

Lean peeking, often called jiggle peeking, is the most basic and controlled way to check an angle. You stay behind cover, then lean just enough while ADS’d to spot the enemy without exposing your full body. It’s clean and predictable, but due to inertia changes, it’s less effective than it used to be. Still, it’s a useful tool for tight spaces or situations where you only need to confirm presence without moving far from safety.

### Circle Peeking for Intel

Circle peeking builds directly on the movement principles from the inertia tutorial. Instead of using a standard A-D strafe (which causes you to stall mid-exposure due to inertia), you use circular movement to stay fluid while gathering info. Start by practicing behind cover, slowly shifting your circular path until the outer edge just exposes you. Once that feels natural, begin adding leans. The lean extends your vision slightly past cover without revealing your full body, letting you see the enemy while keeping nearly your entire model protected. The objective is to maintain constant motion, exposing only what’s necessary to gain information, then gliding smoothly back into cover.

Keep your momentum constant. The key is to flow around the corner and back without stopping, maintaining control over your movement instead of freezing mid-peek. It’s safer than static leaning and provides far more visual information without committing to a full swing.

### Wide Swinging for Momentum Fights

Wide swinging is a high-risk, high-reward technique used to break enemy crosshair discipline and timing. Instead of inching out of cover, you commit to a full-body swing across the angle, forcing the opponent to rapidly adjust their aim. To enhance the speed of your swing, build momentum first by sprinting into a wall and moving your mouse side to side to maintain the sprint. When you push off into the swing, your character carries that momentum, making your movement harder to track. Wide swings are often the better option over trying to hold a tight left-hand angle, since the left-hand peek’s exposure disadvantage makes holding far riskier. Expect enemies to use this frequently when they’re stuck on a left-hand position.

### Jump Peeking for Vision and Safety

Jump peeking is the next evolution of intel gathering. Sprint toward a doorway or angle and jump across while holding freelook toward the potential enemy position. This allows you to spot targets while your head moves both horizontally and vertically, making it extremely difficult for them to land a clean shot.

Because your head height is constantly shifting, you’re never a stationary target. You’ll get a visual read on the enemy’s location and gear while staying unpredictable. These are extremely useful for when you're caught on a left-hand angle. They allow you to reposition to a right-hand angle while not being as dangerous as plain sprinting across the angle.

### Breaking Predictability

Sequentially peeking the same spot is one of the most common mistakes. Once an enemy sees your first peek, they’ll start pre-aiming your head height for the next one. The solution is to mix up your timing and your peeks. Alternate between peeks, angles, and head heights to fight their expectations. Even a half-second timing difference or change in head level can win the engagement.

To make this unpredictability more effective, start chaining different peek types together. After performing a standard circle peek, follow it up with a crouch on your next peek. Changing your head height forces the enemy to adjust their crosshair from your previous position, breaking their pre-aim and creating a brief window where you’re unpredictable. This sudden elevation change often causes them to over- or under-aim just long enough for you to land the first shot. As an added bonus, many optics have thick lower sight frames that block visibility toward your new crouched head level, further reducing their ability to see you during the peek.

While not directly related to predictability, visibility management plays a major role in staying alive during retreats or reloads. When backing away or reloading behind cover, look toward the ground as you move so your head hitbox appears smaller from the enemy’s view.

### Key Takeaways

Peeking is how you collect information without losing fights. Circle peeks give minimal exposure while confirming presence. Jump peeks add vertical unpredictability and let you transition into better positions like right-hand corners. Mixing crouch and circle peeks prevents enemies from pre-aiming your head height. The goal is to reveal as little of yourself as possible while locating the enemy, control when and how you’re seen, and make every movement deliberate before committing to a shot.
