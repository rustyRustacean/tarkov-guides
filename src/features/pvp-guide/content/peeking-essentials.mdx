Fair warning: this is easily the longest chapter in this guide. Peeking touches almost everything else you'll learn: cover discipline, unpredictability, and the timing quirks of Tarkov's netcode all live here, and every other PvP chapter assumes you've internalized this one first.

Staying alive in Tarkov PvP comes down to one habit above all others: stay behind hard cover as much as you possibly can, and expose as little of yourself as possible every single time you have to leave it. Peeking is how you break that rule on purpose (carefully, and only for as long as it takes to get information or a shot).

## Right-Hand vs. Left-Hand Peeking

Peek a right-hand corner and you only need to expose your gun and a sliver of your head before you can see & shoot down the angle. Peek a left-hand corner and the math flips against you: your character's left eye clears the doorframe well before your right eye does (our PMCs see out of our right eye), which means the enemy sees you before you can see them. You're still giving up a massive amount of cover protection even if you're holding the tightest left-hand angle possible. This includes shoulder-swapping, which we'll discuss in the [next section](#shoulder-transitions).

**Takeaway: Default to right-hand angles whenever you have a choice, and treat any left-hand peeks as last resorts.** If you're ever forced onto a left-hand corner, a slow ADS peek isn't the answer due to the aforementioned body exposure, so you're almost always better off swinging it wide with momentum instead of holding it; more on that in the [Wide Swings section below](#wide-swings-committing-with-momentum).

## Shoulder Transitions

A shoulder transition (swapping your gun over without actually changing which hand you're peeking from) is only meant for when you're absolutely required to hold a static left-hand angle. **In reality, I find I almost never use it:** needing to sit on a left hand at all is usually a sign you made a mistake earlier, since proper play would've gotten you a right-hand angle instead.

When it is warranted, a shoulder transition does give you a tighter angle than a normal left-hand peek, but it's still not as efficient as an actual right-hand angle since it doesn't actually swap your hands. It also shifts your crosshair position while point firing, which throws off muscle memory, and it increases your recoil. Worst of all, stuffing your barrel into a wall mid-transition has a clunky effect where the gun temporarily snaps back to your right side, creating a delay while you wait for it to swing back to the left hand. That's why I almost never use this. But yeah, now you know it exists.

## Staying Unpredictable

Peek the same corner the same way more than once and you're training your enemy. The first time you lean out at standing head height, they clock it. The second time, they're often already pre-aiming that exact spot before you get there.

This is where the [Gathering Intel & Baiting](/pvp-guide/gathering-intel) chapter comes in: it covers both learning where the enemy is with minimal exposure and forcing them to give up their position when they're not giving you anything on their own. Lean on it and you should rarely need to peek an angle blind; by the time you actually commit to a peek, you usually already know roughly where they are and you're just confirming or taking the shot. That leaves peeking itself for the moments you can't avoid it, which is exactly when unpredictability matters most.

This same principle extends beyond the corner you're peeking. How you rotate between positions matters just as much. Taking an uncommon route (jump on the stair railings when going up certain stairways), or crossing an open area from an angle most players wouldn't bother with makes you far harder to predict and prefire. Just don't let the pursuit of an unpredictable rotation override good cover discipline. The goal is balance: mix up your paths, but never at the expense of the cover that's actually keeping you alive.

## Jiggle Peeking: Circular Movement Applied

This is the default way you should be peeking most angles, and it's a direct extension of the circular strafing you learned in the movement chapter.

The full procedure:

1. Circle fully behind cover: stay completely hidden while you build up the same smooth, stall-free movement from the [circle strafing chapter](/pvp-guide/circle-strafing).
2. Add a lean right at the edge of the circle to peek out from behind cover.
3. Add a counter-lean on your way back in to speed up the return into cover instead of retracing the same arc.

If you're new to these mechanics, start your practice without leans. Once you're comfortable with the basic rhythm, tighten it up: lean out, take your shot, lean back in, step fully into cover, then decide if a repeek or a short, deliberate pause is required. That pause matters more than it feels like it should. Sometimes enemies will shoot at your first peek late, causing you to peek into their fire. Other times you'll need a second to react to any new information you gathered on your last peek. Also discussed later on, vary your head height on each repeat rather than peeking from the same elevation twice.

One more thing, and it applies to every peek in this guide, not just this one: **always aim at head height while you're peeking.** If your crosshair is already sitting where the head will be, you can fire the instant you see them. If it isn't, you're wasting the first fraction of a second of every exposure just dragging your aim up. The [Crosshair Placement](/pvp-guide/crosshair-placement) chapter covers why this matters so much in more depth.

## Changing Head Height Behind Cover

The reason jiggle peeking works is that you're minimizing your exposure time. When done properly, the enemy only sees you for a few frames at a time, often leading to quite minimal risk given proper peek timings. In addition to timing, being unpredictable is equally as important. Varying your head height is one of the biggest variables you control. Standing, crouching, and (rarely) proning all put your head in a different spot, which means an enemy who pre-aimed your last peek's height is aiming at empty air on the next one.

The critical part: **make the height change while you're still behind cover**, not as part of the peek itself. If you stand up while you're already exposed, the enemy sees the transition and adjusts in real time. Change it behind cover and your next peek is just different, with no warning attached.

One gear note worth knowing here: large-housing sights like EOTechs can physically block your view of a crouched head position through the optic. That's not a reason to avoid those sights (I almost only use the UH-1 personally) just be aware of the risk and double check your sight picture before you commit to a crouched peek with one mounted.

## Wide Swings: Committing With Momentum

Sometimes jiggle peeking isn't the right tool at all & you need to commit and sprint into the open. That's a wide swing: a full push through an opening rather than a controlled lean.

A slow walking swing is close to useless as you're exposed for a long time and moving at a speed that's trivial to track. What you actually want is momentum. Sprint speed takes about 5 seconds to build up to its max, so the highest-momentum version of this (often dubbed a "Ferrari peek") has you sprinting into a wall while moving your mouse left and right so the sprint never gets halted, then carrying that built-up speed straight into the swing. If that's more setup than you need, just tap sprint right before you swing instead; it's usually only slightly slower than a full Ferrari peek and good enough the vast majority of the time from my experience.

Whichever version you use, **stop sprinting the instant you leave cover.** Your gun stays lowered while you're sprinting, and if you're still sprinting when you become visible, you're exposed with no way to shoot back. Sprint-to-ADS also carries a forced delay these days, which makes this trickier than it used to be. Even so, it's still worth doing. This is due to the momentum forcing the enemy into a horizontal flick even if they were already pre-aiming your angle. Most people pre-aim too close to the wall, so a fast-moving target crosses right past where their crosshair is sitting. But the tradeoff is real, as you're now fully exposed with nowhere close to retreat to if you miss your first spray.

This is also an extremely common answer for left-hand corners. Since holding a tight left peek forces you to expose your whole core before your gun even clears cover, swinging wide with momentum is almost always the better option there instead of trying to hold it.

### Desync, and Deciding When to Swing

Desync is the delay between you pressing a key, the server, and then the other player, actually registering it. It varies with server performance and ping, but you can generally expect somewhere in the 0.2-0.7s range. In practice, that means a player who commits to a swing gets a free window of similar length to shoot before a stationary enemy even reacts to their movement. That's where the phrase "swing or get swung" comes from.

A big part of PvP decision-making is figuring out which side of that exchange you want to be on. In general:

- **Swing** when you're stuck on a bad or left-hand angle, or when the enemy already knows exactly where you are and you don't have a better angle to fall back to. A counterswing can sometimes be enough for the desync to become relatively equal between the players.
- **Hold your angle** when you're on a strong, minimally-exposed right-hand position, especially if the enemy is the one stuck on a left-hand angle, and even more so if they don't know you're there at all.

## Peeking Against AI

Everything above applies just as much against AI but with one extra wrinkle: AI accuracy scales up the longer, and the more of your body, they can see. Tight right-hand angles and a fast return to full cover matter even more here than they do against other players.

AI also run through aggression periods. After you peek one (especially after a failed peek where they spot you but you don't get the kill) they go into a window of heightened accuracy and danger. Re-peeking the same AI immediately after a couple of failed attempts is a good way to eat a shot you didn't expect. Back off, let a few seconds pass, and let their aggression settle before you try again.

**Remember:** everything in this chapter comes back to the same three levers: staying behind cover, staying unpredictable, and understanding the few hundred milliseconds of desync that decide whether you should be the one swinging or the one holding. Jiggle peek by default, change your height behind cover so your next peek never matches your last one, and save the wide swing for when you actually need to commit. Get comfortable weighing those three against each other and the rest of this guide starts clicking into place around them.
